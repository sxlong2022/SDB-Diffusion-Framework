**Title:** Hybrid Spatio-Temporal Generative Diffusion Model for Satellite-Derived Marine Bathymetry using Multi-Source Data Fusion

**2. Study Area and Datasets**
    *   2.1. Study Area Description

The study area is centered around Majishan Island, located approximately 1.5 km southwest of Sijiao Island, Shengsi County, within the Zhoushan Archipelago, Zhejiang Province, China (Figure 1). Its approximate geographical coordinates are 122°25' E longitude and 30°40' N latitude. Majishan Island lies about 31 nautical miles from Luchao Port in Shanghai and sits strategically along the essential shipping routes connecting the Yangtze River Delta region, including the Port of Shanghai, to the East China Sea and beyond.

The waters surrounding Majishan Island represent a typical complex coastal island environment within the archipelago. The island is exposed to the open sea on three sides, resulting in relatively open waters characterized by strong tidal currents, particularly near the main channel west of the island. While partially sheltered by neighboring islands, the area features complex hydrodynamics influenced by nearby shallows and the island's topography. The region also hosts significant port infrastructure, including the Baosteel Majishan Terminal, which is located on the southern side of the island. In this study, we focus on the broader coastal and offshore waters surrounding Majishan Island, which are characterized by dynamic hydrodynamic conditions and are representative of the complex environments commonly encountered in coastal bathymetric mapping. The strategic location and environmental complexity of this area make it an ideal and challenging testbed for advanced Satellite-Derived Bathymetry techniques.

    *   2.2. Sentinel-2 Data

Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery constitutes the primary optical remote sensing dataset for this study, providing the foundation for our bathymetric mapping approach. We utilized Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance products from the Copernicus program, accessed through the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform via the 'COPERNICUS/S2_HARMONIZED' collection. The temporal coverage spans from 2018 to 2023, providing a six-year time series that enables both robust multi-temporal analysis and the assessment of potential yearly variations in bathymetric conditions.

For each year in the study period, we filtered the image collection to include only scenes with cloud cover less than 50% (as indicated by the 'CLOUDY_PIXEL_PERCENTAGE' metadata field) within our area of interest (AOI). While Sentinel-2 provides 13 spectral bands at varying spatial resolutions (10m, 20m, and 60m), we primarily focused on four key bands for our preprocessing and analysis: Blue (B2, 490nm), Green (B3, 560nm), Red (B4, 665nm), and Near-Infrared (B8, 842nm), all at 10m native resolution. These bands were specifically selected based on their established relevance to bathymetric applications, with blue and green bands being particularly responsive to water depth variations in coastal environments due to their greater water penetration capabilities.

To ensure consistent spatial registration across all images, we implemented a standardized projection workflow. This process used the B8 band as a reference, extracting its projection information and then resampling all bands to this common 10m grid using bilinear interpolation. This step was crucial for maintaining spatial alignment across the multi-temporal dataset and mitigating potential errors in subsequent analyses due to registration discrepancies.

For enhancing data quality, particularly critical for underwater light penetration analysis, we applied a rigorous atmospheric correction procedure. We implemented the Sensor Invariant Atmospheric Correction (SIAC) method, adapted from Yin et al. (2022), which accounts for multiple atmospheric parameters derived from ancillary datasets, including aerosol optical depth (from MODIS MCD19A2), water vapor (from NCEP), and ozone content (from TOMS). Additionally, topographic effects were addressed using SRTM digital elevation data, accounting for slope and aspect influences. This comprehensive atmospheric correction approach significantly improved the radiometric quality of the imagery, particularly over water bodies where subtle reflectance variations are key indicators of bathymetric features.

    *   2.3. Preprocessing of Sentinel-2 Data

The preprocessing of Sentinel-2 data involved several critical steps designed to optimize the imagery for bathymetric analysis. After atmospheric correction, we implemented a robust cloud and shadow masking procedure leveraging Google's Cloud Score+ algorithm, which provides more accurate cloud detection compared to standard approaches. Only pixels with a Cloud Score+ (CS) value above 0.60 were retained, ensuring minimal cloud contamination while maintaining sufficient data coverage for analysis.

A key innovation in our preprocessing workflow was the application of the Multi-temporal Image Weighted Composition (MIWC) method (Duan et al., 2024), specifically adapted to enhance water penetration signals in optically complex coastal waters. Due to the complexity and unpredictability of remote sensing imaging processes, satellite imagery inevitably contains various noise elements including clouds, haze, and sun glint that can significantly interfere with bathymetric signals. Based on the established positive correlation between visible and near-infrared (NIR) band reflectance over water bodies, our implementation of the MIWC method consists of the following interconnected components:

1. **Negative Pixel Masking**: An initial filtering step that removes negative reflectance values across all bands. These physically impossible negative values typically indicate sensor artifacts or atmospheric correction errors. Our implementation creates band-specific masks and then applies a minimum reducer to generate a combined mask that preserves only valid positive reflectance pixels.

2. **Seasonal Water Quality Adjustment**: A critical enhancement to address seasonal variations in water optical properties that can affect bathymetric signal consistency. This adjustment calculates the Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI) between NIR and Green bands, and then creates a dynamic adjustment factor (1 + NDWI) that is applied to all spectral bands. This approach effectively normalizes reflectance values across different seasons, compensating for variations in turbidity, algal blooms, and other seasonal water quality parameters.

3. **Common Threshold Calculation**: Rather than using iterative Otsu thresholding from the original method, we adapted a more robust approach that calculates a common water segmentation threshold using the 5th percentile of NIR values across the entire image collection. This data-driven approach effectively separates water from non-water features while maintaining consistency across the multi-temporal dataset.

4. **NIR Lower Limit Determination**: For each image in the collection, we identify the minimum NIR reflectance value over water bodies. This lower limit serves as a reference point for the subsequent weighting process, providing a baseline for assessing water clarity.

5. **Weighted Composition Function**: The core of our MIWC implementation calculates pixel-specific weights based on NIR reflectance values. Following our implementation in the code, pixels with lower NIR values (clearer water) receive exponentially higher weights according to:
   
   W(x,y) = [1.0 - (R^NIR(x,y) - R^NIR_min)/(T_common - R^NIR_min)]^p
   
   Where R^NIR(x,y) is the NIR reflectance at pixel (x,y), R^NIR_min is the NIR lower limit, T_common is the common threshold, and p is the power parameter (set to 4 in our implementation). These weights are then used to create a weighted sum of each band's reflectance values across the temporal stack. The weighting scheme effectively emphasizes images with optimal water clarity while suppressing those affected by turbidity or residual clouds.

6. **Band-specific Processing**: While our implementation processes all four bands (Blue, Green, Red, NIR) through the weighted composition process, we primarily focus on the Blue and Green bands for the subsequent bathymetric modeling, as these wavelengths demonstrate the strongest correlation with water depth in coastal environments.

7. **Spatial Gap Filling**: To address data gaps resulting from persistent cloud cover or other quality issues, we implemented a hole-interpolation algorithm that unmasks pixels, applies a focal mean filter with a 2-pixel radius circular kernel, and then selectively fills only the masked regions.

8. **Final Smoothing**: A mean filtering step (1-pixel radius) was applied to reduce pixel-level noise while preserving meaningful bathymetric features.

The MIWC processing resulted in annual Blue and Green band composites with significantly enhanced water penetration, reduced atmospheric artifacts, and improved spatial continuity. These optimized bands were subsequently resampled to the target 64×64 grid resolution using bilinear interpolation to match our computational framework's requirements, and stored as a standardized time series data structure. The MIWC-processed Blue and Green bands serve as the primary input features for the bathymetric estimation model, providing crucial spectral information that correlates strongly with water depth in shallow coastal environments.

This advanced preprocessing workflow ensures that the inherent complexity of coastal waters around Majishan Island—including variable turbidity, bottom reflectance heterogeneity, and seasonal changes—is systematically addressed before the bathymetric modeling stage. The effectiveness of these procedures is evident in the significant improvement in water penetration and feature clarity compared to standard Sentinel-2 processing approaches.

    *   2.4. Nautical Chart Data

Nautical chart data constitutes the primary ground-truth reference for our bathymetric analysis, providing sparse but highly accurate depth measurements obtained through traditional hydrographic surveys. For this study, we utilized official nautical chart data from the Zhoushan Archipelago region, specifically sourced from the Maritime Safety Administration (MSA) of China. Unlike our satellite imagery which spans a six-year period (2018-2023), we only had access to nautical chart data from a single time point (2023), which we used as the reference for both model training and validation across the entire time series.

The nautical chart data is structured in XYZ format, where X and Y represent geographic coordinates (longitude and latitude in WGS84 datum) and Z represents the measured water depth in meters, referenced to the theoretical lowest astronomical tide (LAT).

The preparation of the nautical chart data involved several processing steps to ensure compatibility with our computational framework:

1.  **Spatial Filtering**: We first extracted only those sounding points falling within our defined study area boundary (122.35°E-122.6°E, 30.62°N-30.8°N), resulting in an optimized subset of 461 relevant soundings.

2.  **Depth Validation**: Water depths were verified for physical plausibility, with special attention to shallow measurements that might represent exposed features at low tide. Following standard hydrographic conventions, all depths were converted to positive values, where larger values indicate deeper water.

3.  **Coordinate Normalization**: Geographic coordinates (longitude and latitude) were normalized to the [0,1] range within our study area, facilitating their integration with gridded remote sensing data and computational models. This normalization preserved the precise relative positions of measurements while mapping them to our standardized computational grid.

4. **Measurement Grid Mapping**: For integration with gridded data and models, each filtered sounding point was mapped to its nearest grid cell in our 64×64 computational domain. This process resulted in 442 discrete grid cells containing direct measurements, representing approximately 11% coverage of the domain. The spatial distribution of these measurement cells exhibited characteristic patterns of hydrographic surveys, often denser along navigation routes and near potential hazards, while maintaining more uniform spacing in open waters.

5.  **Statistical Characterization**: We calculated key statistical properties of the depth distribution from the 461 filtered points, including the minimum (0.1m), maximum (75.0m), median (13.8m), interquartile range (15.5m), and depth percentiles (Q25=5.5m, Q75=21.0m), which served as important reference points for data normalization and quality assessment.

The nautical chart data played three crucial roles in our study. First, it provided the ground-truth depths used for training the bathymetric estimation models, establishing the fundamental relationship between optical reflectance patterns and actual water depths. Second, it served as sparse but highly reliable conditioning points for guiding our hybrid modeling approach, acting as "anchors" that constrain the solution space. Third, it provided an independent validation dataset for quantitatively assessing model performance through standard metrics like R², RMSE, and MAE. In our implementation, all available nautical chart points were used simultaneously for training/conditioning and for final validation.

Despite the high accuracy of individual sounding measurements (typically ±0.1m for depths <20m and ±0.5m for greater depths), the sparse nature of these points represents a fundamental challenge for continuous bathymetric mapping. Bridging the gap between these precise but scattered depth measurements and the continuous spatial coverage of satellite imagery forms the core motivation for our hybrid modeling approach.

    *   2.5. GEBCO Global Bathymetry Data

Global bathymetry data from the General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans (GEBCO) provided essential background information for this study. We utilized annual GEBCO grids, specifically the GEBCO_2019 through GEBCO_2024 releases provided by the GEBCO Compilation Group, corresponding temporally to our 2018-2023 Sentinel-2 observation period. Data for the specific study area (122.35°E-122.6°E, 30.62°N-30.8°N) was acquired in NetCDF format.

This dataset offers spatially continuous bathymetric information, complementing the high-resolution optical imagery and the sparse, high-accuracy nautical chart soundings. Analysis of the Type Identifier (TID) grid associated with the GEBCO data revealed the nature of the underlying data sources within our study area. Early years (e.g., 2019-2021) were dominated by depths predicted from satellite gravity data (TID 40) and soundings interpolated using gravity data (TID 44). In later years (e.g., 2022, 2024), a significant portion of the grid cells were derived from pre-generated grids based on mixed source types (TID 70), alongside contributions from direct measurements (TID 17) in 2024. This highlights the evolving nature of the GEBCO product and its general reliance on indirect estimation methods in this region compared to the direct measurements of the nautical charts.

Preprocessing of the GEBCO data involved several steps to prepare it for analysis alongside other datasets. First, the 'elevation' variable was extracted from the NetCDF files. Following standard oceanographic convention, negative elevation values (representing depths below sea level) were converted to positive water depths, while non-negative values (representing land) were identified. The data was then resampled from its native resolution to the target 64×64 grid using bilinear interpolation to ensure spatial alignment. In our study area (ROI), the GEBCO water depth values ranged from 0.00 m to 87.38 m. This processing resulted in an annually resolved GEBCO time series aligned to the common grid.

    *   2.6. JRC Global Surface Water Data

To enhance the visualization of our final bathymetry products, a static land/water mask was derived from the Joint Research Centre (JRC) Global Surface Water (GSW) dataset, accessed via Google Earth Engine. Specifically, we utilized the 'occurrence' band from the GSW1_4 product (Pekel et al., 2016), which provides estimates of water surface presence frequency between 1984 and 2021 at a 30-meter resolution.

For our study area, the GSW occurrence data was processed to create a binary mask. A threshold was applied (e.g., pixels with water occurrence greater than 50% were classified as permanent water, value 1) to distinguish stable water bodies from land areas (value 0). This resulting binary raster was then resampled to our target 64x64 grid resolution using nearest-neighbor interpolation to preserve the crisp land-water boundaries.

It is important to note that this GSW-derived mask was used *exclusively* for visualization purposes in Section 4 (Results). It served to overlay clear land contours onto the generated bathymetry maps, aiding in their interpretation by visually separating terrestrial features from the marine environment. This dataset was not used as an input or constraint for either the classic Random Forest model or the S3GM bathymetry estimation process.

In summary, all datasets utilized in this study—Sentinel-2 derived features, nautical chart soundings, GEBCO bathymetry, and the JRC land/water mask—were meticulously preprocessed and aligned to a common computational grid. This target grid covers the study area (122.35°E-122.6°E, 30.62°N-30.8°N) with a resolution of 64×64 pixels in the WGS84 geographic coordinate system (EPSG:4326). Appropriate resampling techniques (bilinear or nearest-neighbor interpolation) were employed for raster datasets, while point data was mapped to the corresponding grid cells, ensuring spatial consistency crucial for the subsequent modeling stages.

**3. Methodology: The Hybrid S3GM-Bathymetry Framework**

To address the challenges of accurate and spatially coherent Satellite-Derived Bathymetry (SDB) in complex coastal environments, we propose a novel hybrid modeling framework. This framework synergistically combines the strengths of classic machine learning with a state-of-the-art generative deep learning model, specifically designed to effectively fuse multi-source geospatial data over time. The core components involve an initial bathymetry estimation using a Random Forest (RF) model, followed by refinement and spatio-temporal generation using a conditional Spatio-Temporal Generative Diffusion Model (S3GM).

    *   3.1. Overall Architecture

The architecture of the proposed Hybrid S3GM-Bathymetry framework is depicted in Figure 2. It comprises a multi-stage workflow designed to progressively integrate information from diverse data sources towards a final, high-quality bathymetry product. The key stages are:

**Figure 2. Overall architecture of the Hybrid S3GM-Bathymetry framework.** The workflow illustrates the integration of multi-source data (Sentinel-2 imagery, GEBCO bathymetry, nautical charts, and JRC GSW data) through a sequence of processing stages. Data flows from top to bottom, with blue arrows highlighting the primary processing pathway. The framework consists of five main components: (1) data acquisition and preprocessing, which prepares all data sources for subsequent modeling; (2) Stage 1: classic machine learning via Random Forest, which generates preliminary bathymetry estimates (*D<sub>RF</sub>*) from optical features; (3) S3GM input preparation, where all data are normalized and structured into appropriate tensor formats; (4) Stage 2: S3GM processing, comprising pre-training to learn spatio-temporal patterns followed by conditional sampling guided by sparse chart measurements; and (5) denormalization and validation, which converts the generated bathymetry back to physical units and quantitatively assesses the results. This hybrid approach combines the strengths of classic machine learning and modern generative diffusion models to achieve superior bathymetric mapping.

1.  **Data Acquisition and Preprocessing:** This initial stage, detailed in Section 2, involves acquiring and preparing all necessary datasets, including multi-temporal Sentinel-2 imagery, GEBCO global bathymetry grids, nautical chart soundings, and the JRC land/water mask. Crucial preprocessing steps like atmospheric correction (SIAC), multi-temporal weighted composition (MIWC) for Sentinel-2, data filtering, and alignment to a common 64x64 grid are performed here.

2.  **Classic Model Prediction (Stage 1):** A Random Forest (RF) model is trained using the preprocessed Sentinel-2 data, specifically the annual Blue (B2) and Green (B3) band composites derived from the MIWC method (Section 2.3). To improve predictive performance, several engineered features were derived from these bands, including standardized reflectances, the standardized logarithm of the band ratio, individual band logarithms, and a normalized difference index between the standardized bands, resulting in seven input features per pixel.

The RF model was trained using sparse, high-accuracy depth soundings from the official nautical chart data (Section 2.4) corresponding to the year 2023. Only soundings within a valid depth range (0.1m to 75.0m) and located within the study grid were used as the training target. The model learns the empirical relationship between the seven engineered optical features and these ground-truth depths.

We implemented the RF model using the scikit-learn library, configuring it with 500 estimators, a maximum depth of 25, and using the 'sqrt' heuristic for feature consideration at each split. This configuration aimed to balance predictive accuracy with computational load and allowed for out-of-bag error estimation during training. After training on the 2023 data, the model was applied to the MIWC-processed Sentinel-2 data for each year from 2018 to 2023, producing the time series of preliminary bathymetry maps \( D_{RF} \). These maps serve as a key spatially explicit input for the subsequent S3GM stage, providing initial estimates derived directly from the optical data.

    *   3.3. Stage 2: S3GM for Spatio-Temporal Bathymetry Generation

The core of our hybrid framework utilizes a Spatio-Temporal Generative Diffusion Model (S3GM) to refine the initial bathymetry estimates and fuse information from multiple sources over the six-year period (2018-2023). This stage leverages the power of diffusion models to learn complex spatio-temporal distributions and perform conditional generation guided by observations.

        *   3.3.1. S3GM Architecture Overview

Our implementation is based on the S3GM architecture proposed by Yin et al. [1], which employs a U-Net variant specifically designed for video or sequential data (often referred to as Video U-Net). This architecture incorporates temporal attention mechanisms alongside spatial convolutions, enabling it to effectively model correlations both within each annual bathymetry map and across the temporal dimension (i.e., year-to-year variations). This capacity to capture spatio-temporal dynamics is crucial for generating coherent and physically plausible bathymetry time series. The model learns to progressively denoise an initial noise map, guided by the learned score of the data distribution (as detailed in Section 3.3.3).

        *   3.3.2. Input Tensor Formulation

To effectively fuse the diverse datasets within the S3GM, we construct a multi-channel spatio-temporal input tensor. This tensor has dimensions \( [T \times C \times H \times W] \), where \( T=6 \) represents the number of years (2018-2023), \( C \) is the number of input channels, and \( H=64, W=64 \) are the spatial dimensions of our study grid. For our specific application, we define \( C=5 \) channels, each representing a different data source or derived information:

1.  **RF Bathymetry (\( D_{RF} \)):** The preliminary bathymetry maps generated by the Random Forest model in Stage 1. This channel provides a spatially continuous, optically-derived estimate of depth for each year.
2.  **GEBCO Bathymetry (\( D_{GEBCO} \)):** The annual GEBCO bathymetry grids, resampled to the study grid. This channel provides global-scale prior information on water depth.
3.  **Chart Depth Grid:** A sparse grid containing the normalized nautical chart depth values at their corresponding grid cell locations. Cells without measurements are filled with a placeholder value handled during normalization (e.g., a value outside the [-1, 1] range).
4.  **Chart Location Mask:** A binary mask indicating the grid cells where valid nautical chart measurements exist (value 1) and where they do not (value 0). This channel explicitly informs the model about the locations of high-fidelity observations used for conditioning.
5.  **Unified Mask:** A mask designed to help the model differentiate between distinct region types within the grid. It assigns specific values to distinguish land pixels (identified during preprocessing and assigned a distinct value during normalization) from water pixels, providing explicit spatial context about land-water boundaries.

Before these channels were combined into the final input tensor, the normalized RF Bathymetry channel (\( D_{RF} \)) and the normalized GEBCO Bathymetry channel (\( D_{GEBCO} \)) were explicitly weighted with factors of 0.7 and 0.3, respectively. This weighting strategy aimed to leverage the complementary strengths of these data sources, recognizing that optical-derived RF models often excel in shallower waters where bottom signal is strong [Ref_RF_Shallow], while the global GEBCO product can provide a useful prior, particularly in deeper areas where optical signals attenuate [Ref_GEBCO_Deep]. This multi-channel tensor thus integrates the strengths of each data source: the spatial detail from RF (weighted higher), the global context from GEBCO (weighted lower), and the high-accuracy point constraints from the nautical charts, structured in a format suitable for the S3GM's spatio-temporal processing. All input data channels undergo normalization before being fed into the model, as described in Section 3.3.4.

        *   3.3.3. Diffusion Process

The S3GM operates based on the principles of score-based generative modeling through diffusion processes. Specifically, we employ a Variance Preserving Stochastic Differential Equation (VP-SDE) formulation [2]. This process gradually perturbs the input data \( x_0 \) (our multi-channel spatio-temporal tensor) towards a simple prior distribution (typically Gaussian noise) over a continuous time variable \( \tau \in [0, 1] \). The core idea is to train the U-Net model \( \epsilon_\theta(x_\tau, \tau) \) (or its equivalent in v-parameterization) to predict the noise added at time \( \tau \) or a related quantity.

In our implementation, we adopted the v-parameterization for the model output, which has been shown to improve stability and performance in certain regimes [3]. The noise schedule, which dictates the rate of noise addition, is defined by parameters \( \beta_{min} \) and \( \beta_{max} \). Based on empirical findings during model development (detailed in Section 4.3), we set \( \beta_{max} \) to a relatively high value of 1000, which proved critical for stabilizing the training and sampling process for our specific bathymetry data distribution. During generation (sampling), the process is reversed: starting from pure noise \( x_1 \), the model iteratively denoises the sample by solving the corresponding reverse-time SDE, guided by the learned score function, eventually yielding a clean sample \( x_0 \) representing the generated bathymetry time series.

        *   3.3.4. Data Normalization

Consistent data scaling is essential for the stability and performance of diffusion models. Before inputting the multi-channel tensor (Section 3.3.2) into the S3GM, we applied a specific normalization strategy to the relevant channels containing depth information (i.e., \( D_{RF} \), \( D_{GEBCO} \), and the Chart Depth Grid). Although the observed nautical chart depths in our study area range from approximately 0.1m to 75.0m, and the GEBCO data within the ROI spans 0.00m to 87.38m (see Section 2.5), we utilized a slightly wider physical range [\( D_{min}=0 \)m, \( D_{max}=90 \)m] for the Min-Max scaling. This range was chosen to provide headroom for model predictions and to fully encompass the actual value ranges of both chart and GEBCO data, ensuring that most predicted water depths map within the target normalized range [-1, 1] commonly used in diffusion models. The mapping is defined as:

\[ D_{norm} = 2 \times \frac{D_{phys} - D_{min}}{D_{max} - D_{min}} - 1 \]

where \( D_{phys} \) is the physical depth (within the [0, 90]m range considered) and \( D_{norm} \) is the normalized depth. A crucial aspect of this normalization is handling non-water or invalid pixels. Pixels identified as land or containing invalid depth values (e.g., outside the [0, 90]m range, or missing measurements in the Chart Depth Grid) were assigned a distinct placeholder value of 1.5, placing them outside the [-1, 1] range used for valid water depths. This allows the model to explicitly differentiate these regions during processing. The inverse of this transformation, using the same [0, 90]m range, is applied during post-processing (Section 3.1, Stage 5) to convert the model's output back to physical depth units (meters).

        *   3.3.5. Conditional Sampling via Guided Diffusion

While the pre-trained S3GM (Section 3.3.6) learns the general spatio-temporal distribution \( p(x) \) of bathymetry from \( D_{RF} \) and \( D_{GEBCO} \), the goal is to generate samples conditioned on the sparse but high-accuracy nautical chart measurements \( y \), i.e., sampling from the posterior distribution \( p(x | y) \). Here, \( x \) represents the full spatio-temporal bathymetry tensor we wish to generate, and \( y \) represents the conditioning information, specifically the set of normalized nautical chart depths \( y_{chart\_norm} \) at known grid locations \( M \) (provided by the Chart Depth Grid and Chart Location Mask channels).

We achieve this conditioning during the reverse diffusion (sampling) process by modifying the score estimate at each step. A common approach, known as Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS) [5] or similar guidance techniques, approximates the score of the conditional distribution \( \nabla_x \log p_\tau(x|y) \) by combining the score of the unconditional distribution (learned during pre-training) with a guidance term derived from the likelihood \( p_\tau(y|x) \):

\[ \nabla_{x_\tau} \log p_\tau(x_\tau|y) \approx \nabla_{x_\tau} \log p_\tau(x_\tau) + \nabla_{x_\tau} \log p_\tau(y|x_\tau) \]

In practice, the guidance term \( \nabla_{x_\tau} \log p_\tau(y|x_\tau) \) is often implemented by minimizing a loss function at each sampling step \( \tau \) that measures the discrepancy between the current estimated clean sample \( \hat{x}_0^\tau \) (derived from the noisy sample \( x_\tau \) and the model's prediction) and the target observations \( y \).

In our framework, we incorporate two distinct guidance terms to steer the generation process:

1.  **Data Fidelity Loss:** This term ensures that the generated bathymetry aligns with the known nautical chart measurements. It is based on the L1 distance between the estimated clean bathymetry depth channel \( \hat{D}_0^\tau \) (extracted from \( \hat{x}_0^\tau \)) and the normalized chart depths \( y_{chart\_norm} \) at the measurement locations specified by the mask \( M \):
    \[ L_{fidelity} = || M \odot (\hat{D}_0^\tau - y_{chart\_norm}) ||_1 \]
    The influence of this term is controlled by the hyperparameter \( \alpha \). Our experiments revealed that a relatively small value (\( \alpha = 0.1 \)) was crucial for achieving stable and accurate results, preventing the guidance from overpowering the learned prior during sampling (Section 4.3).

2.  **Spatial Smoothness Loss:** To encourage spatially coherent and less noisy bathymetry maps, we introduce a regularization term that penalizes large spatial gradients in the estimated clean depth channel \( \hat{D}_0^\tau \). We use the L1 norm of the horizontal (\( \nabla_w \)) and vertical (\( \nabla_h \)) differences:
    \[ L_{spatial} = || \nabla_h \hat{D}_0^\tau ||_1 + || \nabla_w \hat{D}_0^\tau ||_1 \]
    This term promotes smoother transitions between adjacent depth pixels. Its strength is controlled by the hyperparameter \( \gamma_{spatial} \). We found a small value (\( \gamma_{spatial} = 5 \times 10^{-7} \)) effectively reduced noise while preserving important details (Section 4.3).

The gradients of these loss terms with respect to \( x_\tau \) are calculated at each step of the reverse SDE solver and added to the unconditional score estimate, effectively guiding the sampling trajectory towards solutions that honor both the learned spatio-temporal patterns and the specific constraints imposed by the nautical chart data and the smoothness prior. Specifically, we employed a predictor-corrector sampling scheme based on [2]. In our final configuration, we used a predictor step followed by one corrector step (`inner_loop=1`) per time discretization interval, with a signal-to-noise ratio (`snr`) parameter of 0.01 for the corrector step, balancing sampling efficiency and quality.

        *   3.3.6. Pre-training Strategy

Before performing conditional sampling, the S3GM network was pre-trained in an unsupervised manner to learn the underlying spatio-temporal patterns inherent in the bathymetry data. The pre-training dataset consisted of the multi-channel input tensor (Section 3.3.2) but excluded the chart-specific channels (Chart Depth Grid and Chart Location Mask), primarily focusing on the information from the RF model predictions (\( D_{RF} \)) and the GEBCO data (\( D_{GEBCO} \)). The model was trained using the standard denoising score matching objective associated with the VP-SDE formulation [2], learning to predict the noise (or the v-parameterization equivalent) added during the forward diffusion process. This pre-training phase allows the model to capture the general structure, spatial correlations, and temporal evolution characteristics of bathymetry in the study area, establishing a robust prior distribution before incorporating the specific constraints from the nautical chart data during the conditional sampling stage.

    *   3.4. Implementation Details

The proposed hybrid S3GM-Bathymetry framework was implemented using Python. Data preprocessing, including Sentinel-2 image acquisition and MIWC processing, utilized the Google Earth Engine Python API and libraries such as NumPy and SciPy. The Random Forest model (Stage 1) was implemented using scikit-learn. The core S3GM (Stage 2), including the Video U-Net architecture and the VP-SDE diffusion process with conditional sampling, was built upon the PyTorch deep learning framework. Key Python libraries employed for data handling, processing, and visualization included Joblib (for model saving), Matplotlib, and PIL. Model training and sampling were executed on a local workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2060 GPU to accelerate computations. Specific hyperparameters related to the SDE schedule (\( \beta_{max} \)), guidance weights (\( \alpha \), \( \gamma_{spatial} \)), and sampler settings (`inner_loop`, `snr`) are detailed in Sections 3.3.3, 3.3.5, and further discussed in Section 4.3.

    *   3.5. Evaluation Metrics

To quantitatively assess the performance of the final bathymetry generated by our hybrid S3GM framework, we employed standard regression evaluation metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R²), root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE), by comparing the predicted depths with the ground-truth nautical chart measurements. These widely used metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of the model's accuracy and predictive capability.

    *   4.3. Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis

The performance and stability of conditional diffusion models, particularly when applied to complex regression tasks like SDB using guided sampling, are highly sensitive to hyperparameter settings. Our development process involved systematic investigation into several key parameters governing the VP-SDE noise schedule and the conditional guidance mechanism.

Initial experiments highlighted the critical role of the noise schedule parameter \( \beta_{max} \) within the VP-SDE formulation, especially when using v-parameterization. An early configuration with a lower \( \beta_{max} \) (e.g., 20) resulted in numerical instability during the sampling process, particularly in later stages, leading to divergent predictions and poor performance (R² << 0). Increasing \( \beta_{max} \) substantially to 1000 proved essential for stabilizing the diffusion process and enabling meaningful learning, although it did not solely resolve all convergence issues.

The most significant factor influencing the final accuracy and stability was the data fidelity weight \( \alpha \), which controls the strength of the guidance towards the sparse nautical chart measurements (Section 3.3.5). An initial, seemingly intuitive, high value (\( \alpha = 1.0 \)) resulted in overly strong corrections during sampling. This led to gradient explosion issues, causing the estimated clean bathymetry \( \hat{D}_0^\tau \) to drastically overshoot the target normalization range [-1, 1] and ultimately yielding physically unrealistic results. A critical breakthrough was achieved by drastically reducing this weight to \( \alpha = 0.1 \). This reduction effectively balanced the influence of the learned prior distribution \( p(x) \) and the conditioning signal \( p(y|x) \), allowing the model to adhere to the accurate chart soundings without destabilizing the sampling trajectory. This adjustment was paramount in achieving the high quantitative accuracy reported in Section 4.2.1 (R² ≈ 0.932).

Furthermore, the impact of the spatial smoothness regularization, controlled by \( \gamma_{spatial} \), was investigated. While setting \( \gamma_{spatial} = 0 \) already produced accurate results with \( \alpha = 0.1 \), introducing a small spatial smoothness weight (\( \gamma_{spatial} = 5 \times 10^{-7} \)) yielded a marginal improvement in quantitative metrics and, more importantly, visibly enhanced the spatial coherence of the generated bathymetry maps (as seen in Figure 4b). This small regularization term effectively suppressed residual pixel-level noise apparent in preliminary results without overly smoothing sharp bathymetric features, contributing to the final model's realistic spatial appearance.

These findings underscore that careful and systematic tuning of hyperparameters, particularly the SDE schedule parameter \( \beta_{max} \) and the guidance weights \( \alpha \) and \( \gamma_{spatial} \), is indispensable for the successful application of guided diffusion models like S3GM to complex geospatial inversion problems such as multi-source SDB. The optimal configuration identified through this process was crucial for obtaining the stable, accurate, and spatially coherent results presented.
